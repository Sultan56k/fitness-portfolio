You have trained consistently for six months. You have not missed many sessions. You are lifting roughly what you lifted in month two, and you look roughly how you looked in month two.

This is the most common plateau in training, and it is almost never a motivation problem. It is that nothing about your training has actually got harder.

## Why This Happens

Your body adapts to demands, and then stops. That is the entire mechanism.

When you lift something genuinely challenging, you create a stimulus your body is not currently equipped to handle comfortably. It responds over the following days by building capacity — more force production, more muscle tissue, better neural recruitment. After a few weeks, that same weight is no longer challenging, and your body has no reason to build anything further.

At that point the session still costs you an hour. It still makes you sweat. It is still exercise, and it is still good for your health. But it has stopped being a *stimulus*, because the demand has not moved.

This is why "I train hard" and "I train progressively" are different claims. You can train extremely hard and make no progress for a year if the load never changes. The body responds to *increasing* demand, not to effort in isolation.

## What Most People Get Wrong

**Thinking overload only means adding weight.** This is the belief that stalls people fastest. Weight is one lever of five, and it is the one that runs out soonest — you cannot add 2.5 kg a week indefinitely.

**Adding weight before earning it.** Jumping up 10 kg and grinding out four ugly reps is not progression, it is a form breakdown that raises injury risk while reducing the stimulus to the muscle you were targeting.

**Changing the programme instead of the load.** Six weeks in, progress slows, so people swap to an entirely new routine. Now they are a beginner at five new exercises with no record of what they lifted, and the cycle restarts. **Programme-hopping is the most common substitute for progression.**

**Not recording anything.** You cannot progressively overload what you do not measure. Walking in and trying to remember last Tuesday means defaulting to what feels normal — and what feels normal is, by definition, what you have already adapted to.

> Training hard and training progressively are not the same thing. Plenty of people do the first for years and never do the second.

## The Fix

### The five levers

You only need to move one, this week, and know which one you moved.

**1. Load.** Add weight. The obvious lever, and the first to stall.

**2. Reps.** Same weight, more repetitions. Going from 8 to 12 reps at 60 kg is more total work than adding 5 kg and dropping to 6.

**3. Sets.** Three becomes four. Adds volume without touching the weight or risking your technique.

**4. Tempo.** Lower for three seconds instead of dropping. The same 60 kg becomes considerably harder with nothing added to the bar — and this is the safest lever when training at home with fixed dumbbells.

**5. Range of motion.** A deeper squat, a fuller stretch at the bottom of a row. More range at the same load is more work, and usually better for the joint.

### Double progression: the system to actually use

This gives you a clear instruction every single session, which is why it works.

1. **Pick a rep range** — say 8 to 12
2. **Start at the bottom** with a weight you control for 8 clean reps
3. **Add one rep per session** until you hit 12 across all sets
4. **Add weight, drop back to 8**, and repeat

Four weeks of that:

| Week | Weight | Sets × Reps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 60 kg | 3 × 8 |
| 2 | 60 kg | 3 × 10 |
| 3 | 60 kg | 3 × 12 |
| 4 | 65 kg | 3 × 8 |

You end the month lifting more than you started, for the same reps. That is progress you can prove, not progress you hope is happening.

### Write it down

Exercise, weight, sets, reps. Ten seconds per set in a notes app. This is the highest-return habit in training and the one most commonly skipped — and it is what makes every other point on this page possible.

### When you stall

**One bad session** is usually sleep, food, or stress. Repeat it next time.

**Three sessions stuck on the same lift** means you need a deload. Drop to 85% of your working weight for a week, then build back. You will normally pass the old sticking point within a fortnight.

**Everything stalling at once** is not a programme problem. It is recovery — under-eating, under-sleeping, or training more days than you can absorb. Fix that before changing a single exercise. If fat loss is also the goal, note that [an aggressive deficit will stall your lifts](/blog/how-many-calories-to-lose-weight) long before your programme is at fault.

## For Beginners Specifically

In your first six to twelve months, progress is close to automatic — you can often add weight every session, because you are adapting neurologically as much as building tissue.

Take those gains, keep the reps clean, and resist advanced programming you do not need yet. The lifter who adds a small amount every month for two years ends up far ahead of the one who chases a new routine every six weeks. If you are still in the first month, [start here instead](/blog/beginners-guide-to-strength-training).

Tonight, open a notes app and write down what you lifted. Next session, beat one number on that page — any of the five levers. That is the whole practice.
