You have been told to drink more water to lose weight, probably without any explanation of the mechanism.

Water matters. It matters for real, modest, well-understood reasons — and not for the ones it is usually sold on. Knowing the difference stops you from expecting something it cannot deliver.

## Why This Happens

Water is free, harmless, and easy to recommend, which makes it the most repeated advice in fitness. Repetition has inflated the claims.

The honest position: **water does not burn fat.** There is no metabolic pathway by which drinking more water causes fat tissue to be broken down. Any advice implying otherwise is wrong.

What water genuinely does:

- **Fills the stomach**, which modestly reduces intake at the next meal
- **Replaces liquid calories** if it displaces soft drinks or sweetened chai
- **Supports training quality** — even mild dehydration measurably reduces strength output
- **Prevents thirst being misread as hunger**, which is real but smaller than usually claimed

Those are worthwhile. None of them is dramatic, and stacked together they are a helpful supporting habit rather than a lever.

## What Most People Get Wrong

**Believing the calorie-burning claim.** Drinking cold water does cost energy to warm — roughly 8 calories per glass. Across two litres that is under 40 calories a day. Real, and irrelevant.

**Fixating on a fixed number of litres.** The "eight glasses" figure has no strong evidential basis. Requirements vary with bodyweight, climate, activity, and diet — and a great deal of your intake comes from food.

**Not understanding water weight, which is the big one.** Water shifts cause **1–2 kg of daily scale variation**, entirely independent of fat. High salt, a heavy carbohydrate meal, a hard training session, poor sleep, or the menstrual cycle all cause retention. People read these as fat gain and abandon working plans over them.

**Drinking too much.** Rare but real. Very high intake without electrolytes can dilute blood sodium, which is dangerous. More is not indefinitely better.

> Water will not burn your fat. It will substantially change what the scale says from one morning to the next — which is why so many people quit a plan that was working.

## The Fix

### How much you actually need

A reasonable target: **30–35 ml per kilogram of bodyweight per day**, from all sources including food, tea, and coffee.

| Bodyweight | Daily target |
| --- | --- |
| 60 kg | 1.8–2.1 litres |
| 75 kg | 2.2–2.6 litres |
| 90 kg | 2.7–3.1 litres |

Add roughly 500 ml per hour of hard training, and more in summer heat.

**The simplest check requires no measuring at all: pale straw-coloured urine means you are adequately hydrated.** Dark means drink more. Completely clear all day means you can ease off.

Tea and coffee count. The claim that they dehydrate you is outdated — the caffeine has a mild diuretic effect that does not offset the fluid itself.

### Where it genuinely helps a diet

**A glass before meals.** Modest, but it reliably reduces intake slightly at the meal that follows.

**Replacing liquid calories.** This is the largest real effect by a wide margin. A daily soft drink or two sweetened chais is 200–400 calories that contribute nothing to fullness. Removing them is often a quarter of an entire deficit — and that is a water-related win, even though the water is not what caused it.

**Before and during training.** Dehydration of 2% of bodyweight measurably reduces strength. If you train in Karachi or Lahore in summer, this is not a marginal concern.

### How to read the scale properly

Because water moves 1–2 kg day to day, a single reading is noise.

**Weigh daily under the same conditions — morning, after the toilet, before eating — and compare weekly averages, not individual days.** If this week's average is below last week's, the plan is working, regardless of what Wednesday said.

Expect a jump after a salty meal, after a hard session, or after a high-carbohydrate day. None of it is fat. [This is one of the most common reasons people believe they have plateaued](/blog/weight-loss-plateau-not-willpower) when they have not.

### Hydration in this climate

Standard advice underestimates requirements here for several months of the year.

In summer heat, particularly for anyone working outdoors or training in a non-air-conditioned gym, sweat losses climb sharply. The 30–35 ml per kg guide is a baseline; on a hot day training hard, an additional litre is reasonable.

Two practical points. **Plain water is enough for sessions under about an hour** — sports drinks are unnecessary and mostly sugar. Beyond that, or when sweating heavily, a pinch of salt in water or a simple homemade lemon-salt-sugar drink replaces electrolytes for a fraction of the cost of anything bottled.

During Ramadan the arithmetic changes entirely: the fluid you would normally spread across the day has to fit between iftar and sehri, which takes deliberate planning rather than drinking heavily at one sitting.

## Tracking It Without Thinking About It

Water is one of those habits that works when it is automatic and fails when it depends on remembering.

**FitLife** tracks daily water alongside weight, steps, and sleep, with reminders through the day — which turns it from something you intend to do into something you have done. Seeing water logged next to your weight trend also makes the day-to-day scale noise obvious rather than alarming.

Today, do the honest version: replace one sweetened drink with water. That single swap does more for your deficit than any amount of additional water on top of your current diet.
