You train four times a week and you are stuck. The obvious conclusion is that you need to train harder or more often.

Before you add a fifth session, check your step count. For a great many people that number explains the stall completely, and it is invisible unless you look at it.

## Why This Happens

Your daily energy expenditure has a component almost nobody accounts for: **NEAT**, or non-exercise activity thermogenesis. Walking, standing, cooking, taking stairs, fidgeting — everything that is not deliberate training.

The scale of it surprises people. NEAT can vary by **several hundred calories a day** between two people of the same size, and for most people it is considerably larger than their training burn.

Compare honestly. A hard hour of resistance training is roughly 300–400 calories. Ten thousand steps is roughly 400–500 for an average adult, every day, without a session.

The problem is that NEAT is not a decision, which is what makes it so easy to lose. When you are in a calorie deficit your body quietly conserves energy — you take the lift, you sit sooner, you move less between tasks. Nobody chooses this and almost nobody notices it, and it can erase a substantial part of your deficit within weeks.

## What Most People Get Wrong

**Believing the workout is where the calories go.** Four hard sessions a week is roughly 1,400 calories total — about 200 a day. A step count that drops from 9,000 to 4,000 costs you more than that.

**Trusting machine calorie readouts.** Treadmill and cross-trainer displays routinely overstate by 20–40%, because they cannot know your body composition. Eating back "exercise calories" from those numbers is a quiet way to undo a deficit.

**Treating steps as a substitute for training.** They are not. Walking does not build or preserve muscle, and in a deficit muscle is exactly what you are trying to keep. Steps and resistance training solve different problems.

**Adding cardio instead of adding movement.** An extra gym cardio session is 45 minutes of effort with a recovery cost. Getting off the bus a stop early costs you nothing and can be sustained indefinitely.

> The gym session is what you notice. The other fifteen waking hours are usually where the larger number is, and where a stall hides.

## The Fix

### Establish where you actually are

Before setting a target, measure a normal week. Most people substantially over-estimate their movement, and a desk job with a car commute often produces 3,000–4,000 steps a day without you realising.

Your phone has been counting this already. Check the last month.

### Set a realistic target

| Current daily average | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Under 4,000 | 6,000 |
| 4,000–6,000 | 8,000 |
| 6,000–8,000 | 10,000 |
| Over 10,000 | Hold it |

**Do not jump straight to 10,000 from 3,000.** A target you miss daily gets abandoned within a fortnight. Add roughly 2,000 and hold it until it feels automatic.

There is nothing magical about 10,000 — the figure originated in a Japanese pedometer marketing campaign in the 1960s. The research shows meaningful health benefits accumulating from around 7,000, with returns flattening past roughly 10,000. Anywhere in that band is good.

### Where the steps actually come from

Nobody finds 8,000 steps in one walk. They come from stacking:

- **A 20-minute walk after dinner** — roughly 2,000 steps, and it helps digestion and sleep
- **Taking calls standing or walking** — an easy 1,000–1,500
- **Stairs instead of the lift** — small but daily
- **Parking further away, or one stop early** — 500–1,000
- **A 10-minute walk after lunch** — 1,000

### Watch it during a diet

This is the point most people miss. **When your loss stalls, check your steps before cutting calories.** If they have fallen 3,000 since you started, restoring them fixes the deficit without eating less — which is a far better trade than another 200-calorie cut on top of an existing one.

[This is one of the five usual causes of a plateau](/blog/weight-loss-plateau-not-willpower), and it is the one people check last.

### The local obstacles, honestly

Two things make step targets harder here than the advice usually assumes.

**Walkability varies enormously.** Many areas have no pavements, and walking beside traffic is neither pleasant nor safe. If that describes your neighbourhood, the realistic options are a park, a market, indoor walking, or pacing during phone calls — and none of those is a compromise, they all count identically.

**Summer heat.** For several months, an afternoon walk is not sensible. Shift to early morning or after sunset. A 20-minute walk after dinner is both cooler and better for digestion and sleep.

Neither obstacle changes the target. Both change when and where you hit it.

### Steps are not a replacement for training

Worth restating, because this article can be misread.

Walking does not build muscle, and in a deficit muscle is exactly what you are trying to keep. Someone who walks 12,000 steps daily and never lifts will lose weight and end up smaller with less definition — the outcome most people are actually trying to avoid.

The correct arrangement is both: resistance training two or three times a week to keep the muscle, and a step count high enough to support the deficit without adding recovery cost. They solve different problems and neither substitutes for the other.

Check your average for the last month today. If it has fallen since you started your plan, you have found your problem without touching your diet.
